# Slow leverage, not fast trades, reaches $1,000

No strategy with independently replicated evidence turns $200 into $1,000 quickly or reliably. One strategy has decades of evidence behind it and a real, if slow, chance of getting there, and an automated bot on a $200 Alpaca cash account can run it. The rule is to hold a 3x S&P 500 fund (UPRO) only while the S&P 500 is above its 200-day average, and hold cash otherwise. In the coordinator's backtest over 1993-2026, this rule grew about **16% a year**. From a typical starting day, $200 reached $1,000 within 3 years only **6% of the time**, within 5 years **15% of the time**, and took a median of **7.9 years**. Along the way it lost up to **71%** from a high point. The Nasdaq version (TQQQ) did better on fast doubling: it reached $1,000 within 5 years **30% of the time**. But it lost money for a whole decade (−16% a year over 1999-2007) and fell **95%** at its worst. The bot's current strategy is a fast breakout trade, and the bot can't run it profitably. The bot's own two-year test came out at break-even before costs. The bot pays 0.26-0.48% extra on each buy, which is 10-20 times the cost at which even the published intraday strategies stop making money. The stories of people "easily" flipping $50 into $1,000 are survivors of a lottery. In a fair game, the chance of turning $50 into $1,000 before losing it all is at most 5%, whatever betting method is used. The quickest dependable way to a $1,000 balance is adding money. The trend strategy is a reasonable way to make that money grow afterwards.

## Five terms first, then the arithmetic of 5x

The report uses a handful of terms, defined here once.

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **CAGR** | Compound annual growth rate: the steady yearly growth that would turn the starting amount into the ending amount. 16% CAGR doubles money in about 4.7 years. |
| **Drawdown** | The fall from the account's highest value to its lowest point after that. A −71% drawdown turns $700 into $203. |
| **R** | The amount a trade risks: entry price minus stop price. +0.1R a trade means that, on average, each trade made 10% of what it risked. |
| **Basis point (bp)** | One hundredth of one percent (0.01%). Professional strategies often earn 2-3 bp a trade. |
| **Slippage** | The gap between the price the bot expected and the price it actually got. |
| **3x ETF** | A fund that aims for three times the index's move each day: if the S&P rises 1% in a day, UPRO rises about 3%. |
| **Backtest / out-of-sample** | A backtest replays rules on past prices. Out-of-sample means testing on data the rules were not designed on, for example years after a paper was published. That is the only kind of test that counts as evidence. |

Turning $200 into $1,000 means multiplying the money by five, a +400% gain, and compounding sets a hard speed limit on that. At 10% a year a 5x takes **16.9 years**; at 20% it takes 8.8 years; at 40% it takes 4.8 years; doing it in about 2.3 years requires doubling every single year ([notes backtest output](../research_notes/Small%20account%20trading%20strategy%20evidence/backtest_code/trend_leverage_backtest_output.txt)). The S&P 500 has averaged roughly 10% a year. Any plan to reach $1,000 in a year or two therefore needs returns no published, replicated strategy has delivered.

### Why other people seem to flip $50 into $1,000 easily

Gambling maths explains the stories. In a fair game, where each bet is as likely to win as to lose, the chance of growing a stake to a target before losing it all equals start divided by target. That works out to **20% for $200→$1,000** and **5% for $50→$1,000**, and no betting system or bet size can beat it. With even a slight disadvantage, such as costs or a 49/51 coin, the odds fall further. Betting everything at once gets closest to the ceiling, and small bets do worst ([notes calculation](../research_notes/Small%20account%20trading%20strategy%20evidence/backtest_code/trend_leverage_backtest_output.txt)). The coordinator ran a simulation on the bot's own trade history with no edge and large bets. It gave about a **4% chance of $50→$1,000 and about a 95% chance of going broke**. Of every 25 people trying the "flip", roughly one posts a screenshot and the other 24 stay quiet. Large studies of real traders confirm this. Of Brazilians who day-traded futures for more than 300 days, **97% lost money** and only 1.1% earned more than the minimum wage ([Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3423101)). In 15 years of Taiwanese market data, **fewer than 1% of day traders** predictably beat the market after fees ([Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, J. Financial Markets](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:18:y:2014:i:c:p:1-24)). Retail traders lost about $358,000 a day on same-day S&P options after daily expiries began ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-04-21/day-traders-lose-358-000-per-day-gambling-on-zero-day-options)).

## The bot's day-trading edge is smaller than its own costs

The bot trades a narrow version of the opening-range breakout. It buys stocks already up 4%+ on 1.5x normal opening volume when they break their first-15-minute high, with the stop at the range low and the target at 2R, and it is out by 3:55 PM. Over two years of hourly data that rule made **−0.009R a trade before costs and −0.057R at 0.2% slippage a side**, with a likely range that includes zero ([bot experiments log](../research/EXPERIMENTS.md)). Live, the paper account went from $55 to $49.65 over 30 trades. The cause is measurable. The bot polls delayed Yahoo data every five minutes from GitHub Actions and buys with market orders, so its buys filled **0.26-0.48% worse** than the signal price. A swing-trading variant held for days showed no edge over random entries (+0.01R to +0.05R, against +0.01R for random) on about 950 stocks ([bot experiments log](../research/EXPERIMENTS.md)).

The published research explains why this was always going to be hard. The famous "stocks in play" paper reports a Sharpe ratio of 2.81 for opening-range breakouts on the 20 most unusually active stocks each day ([Zarattini, Barbon & Aziz, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4729284)). (A Sharpe ratio is return per unit of bumpiness; the stock market is around 0.6.) The same breakout without the activity filter made only **3.2% a year** ([QuantifiedStrategies](https://www.quantifiedstrategies.com/stocks-in-play-trading-strategy-day-trading/)). The well-known independent replication covers only 2016, the paper's first year ([CoursIA review](https://github.com/jsboige/CoursIA/issues/16355)). A 2023-2025 test of a similar setup produced a **negative Sharpe of −0.50** ([QuantConnect backtest](https://www.quantconnect.com/terminal/cache/embedded_backtest_01b8fa640b519cbc4a61330ce572c66c.html)). The paper also assumes about $0.02 a share of slippage, roughly 0.1% on a $20 stock, which is a fraction of what the bot pays.

The index-level intraday strategies tell the same story. On QQQ, the 5-minute opening-range breakout loses almost all of its edge with just 2 cents a share of slippage: Sharpe falls from 1.06 to 0.23. About 75% of its trades are stopped out ([Brusco replication](https://github.com/giovannibrusco/zarattini-2023-orb-qqq)). "Beat the Market", a noise-band momentum strategy on SPY, reported 19.6% a year, but that needed up to 4x leverage and shorting. Out-of-sample since May 2024 it made **4.8% a year, worse than just holding SPY** ([Paz Serpa replication](https://github.com/PazSheimy/spy-intraday-momentum-oos)), and recent Sharpe is about zero ([Brusco replication](https://github.com/giovannibrusco/zarattini-2024-momentum-spy)). It stops making money at only **about 2.2 bp of extra slippage** ([francesco-nicolo replication](https://github.com/francesco-nicolo/intraday-momentum-replication)). The older finding that the first half-hour predicts the last half-hour ([Gao et al., JFE 2018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:129:y:2018:i:2:p:394-414)) shows "no tradeable edge" in SPY or QQQ over 2024-2026, even before costs ([independent test](https://github.com/hongfu12321/trading-backtest/pull/3)).

Stock-picking patterns fare no better. The classic short-term reversal effect, buying last week's losers, "has steadily weakened over time, to the point of now having vanished entirely in most regions" ([Blitz et al., SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4575689)). Among heavily traded, news-driven stocks, which are the bot's universe, prices tend to keep moving rather than reverse ([Medhat & Schmeling, CEPR](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP15857.pdf)). Drift after earnings announcements has been absent in large stocks since about 2006 ([Martineau, Critical Finance Review](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/CFR-0122)). Where it survived, in tiny illiquid stocks, costs absorbed **63-100% of the paper profit** ([Chordia et al., SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1403342)).

The conclusion is direct. Every replicated short-term edge is measured in basis points. The bot pays 26-48 basis points on each buy and trades daily. At 0.4% a side, a once-a-day round trip costs about 0.8% a day, roughly 200% a year in drag. A strategy that trades rarely suffers far less from this problem.

## Famous calendar patterns shrink to almost nothing after costs

The overnight effect is the best known of these patterns. It means buying at the close and selling at the next morning's open. Over 1993-2020, nearly all of the US stock market's gain happened overnight ([Lou, Polk & Skouras, JFE](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v134y2019i1p192-213.html)). In the coordinator's backtest, SPY overnight made **10.1% a year with zero costs**, with a smaller worst drop (−33%) than buy-and-hold. It fell to **7.4% at a 0.005% cost per side and −0.4% at 0.02%**. QQQ overnight did better (13.8% at zero cost), but still fell to 2.9% at 0.02% a side ([coordinator backtest, research/classic.py](../research/classic.py)). The effect also faded in recent years: SPY overnight made 10.6% a year in 1993-2007 and only 2.0% in 2008-2015 ([coordinator backtest](../research/classic.py)). The New York Fed reports that its own overnight-drift window has averaged close to zero since 2021 ([Liberty Street Economics, 2026](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/07/the-disappearing-overnight-drift/)). An ETF built to capture the effect returned −6.9% while the S&P rose 22%, and closed in 2023 ([WealthManagement](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/etfs/-night-effect-etfs-to-shut-down-with-overnight-stock-returns-elusive)). There is a practical problem too. Alpaca's opening and closing auction orders are the cheapest way to trade at exact open and close prices, but fractional-share orders appear limited to regular day orders, so a $200 account cannot use those auctions ([Alpaca fractional trading docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/fractional-trading); [Alpaca order types](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/orders-at-alpaca)).

The turn-of-the-month effect means holding stocks only on the last trading day of the month and the first three days of the next. It once captured essentially all of the market's excess return ([McConnell & Xu](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1042&context=ciberwp)). A 30-country study still finds about 10 bp a day in that window over 1994-2023 ([J. Int. Financial Markets, 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1042443126000259)). As a strategy, though, it sits in cash about three-quarters of the time. In the coordinator's backtest it made **3.1% a year on SPY** with a −19% worst drop ([coordinator backtest](../research/classic.py)), and at that rate a 5x takes about 50 years. Pre-holiday strength is real but "markedly less so in recent data" ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/calendar-effects/stock-returns-around-thanksgiving/)). These patterns lower risk. They do not make money grow faster.

## Trend filters plus 3x leverage are the only realistic 5x route

Trend following has the best evidence of any approach here, from independent researchers, many markets and long time spans. It means holding an asset while its price is above a long-run average and moving to cash when it falls below. The original study found it worked in **all 58 futures markets tested** ([Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen, JFE 2012](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:104:y:2012:i:2:p:228-250)). A follow-up found it "consistently profitable" over more than 110 years ([Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen, AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing)). Faber's simple moving-average rule, published in 2007, held up after publication, mainly by stepping aside during 2008 ([Swedroe via ETF.com](https://www.etf.com/sections/index-investor-corner/swedroe-why-financial-trends-persist)). The honest catch is that on a single stock index trend following **lowers risk, not returns**. Over 1928-2026 the S&P with a 200-day filter made 9.8% a year against 9.4% for buy-and-hold, but its worst drop was −51% instead of −85% ([notes backtest output](../research_notes/Small%20account%20trading%20strategy%20evidence/backtest_code/trend_leverage_backtest_output.txt)). It also had a weak 2010s ([AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/You-Cant-Always-Trend-When-You-Want)).

Lower risk is what makes leverage usable. Gayed and Bilello's award-winning study applied the 200-day filter to a leveraged S&P 500 ([CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/volatility-effects/leveraging-the-u-s-stock-market-based-on-sma-rules/)). The idea: a 3x fund grows fast in calm uptrends and is wiped out in long bear markets, and the filter's whole job is to be in cash during those bear markets. It works because 3x funds reset daily, which hurts them in choppy markets. If the index falls 10% and then rises 11.1%, it is back where it started. A 3x fund falls 30% and then rises 33.3%, which leaves it **6.7% down**. In 2026 so far, QQQ is up 3.5% while TQQQ is down 13.6% ([notes backtest output](../research_notes/Small%20account%20trading%20strategy%20evidence/backtest_code/trend_leverage_backtest_output.txt)). Regulators warn that these funds can lose heavily when held longer than a day ([SEC/FINRA investor alert](https://www.investor.gov/news-alerts/investor-alerts/sec-finra-investor-alert-leveraged-inverse-etfs)).

The coordinator's backtest measures the odds of $200 reaching $1,000 from every fifth historical trading day as a starting point. It uses real UPRO and TQQQ prices after their launches and a simulated 3x fund before that, charging the fund's 0.95% fee plus about 6% a year for borrowing. Cash earns nothing, and each trade costs 0.02% ([coordinator backtest](../research/classic.py)).

| Strategy (data from) | CAGR | Worst drop | $1,000 within 3 yrs | within 5 yrs | Median wait | Halved within 5 yrs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hold SPY (1993) | 10.6% | −55% | 0% | 0% | 17.1 yrs | 4% |
| Hold QQQ (1999) | 10.2% | −83% | — | 0% | 11.3 yrs | 12% |
| SPY 200-day trend, 1x | 8.1% | −29% | — | — | — | — |
| Hold 3x S&P | 14.7% | −98% | 10% | 31% | 6.8 yrs | **46%** |
| **SPY 200-day trend into 3x S&P** | **16.0%** | **−71%** | **6%** | **15%** | **7.9 yrs** | **8%** |
| Hold 3x Nasdaq | 1.5% | −100% | — | 52% | — | 45% |
| **QQQ 200-day trend into 3x Nasdaq** | **9.6%** | **−95%** | **5%** | **30%** | **6.1 yrs** | **16%** |

The table shows the trade-off. Holding a 3x fund without a filter gives higher odds of a quick 5x, but about **a 45% chance of being cut in half within five years**, and the 3x Nasdaq fund was effectively wiped out in 2000-2002. Adding the filter cuts the chance of halving to 8% (S&P) or 16% (Nasdaq) and gives up some of the upside. The results also depend heavily on the era. The S&P version made 11.2% a year in 1993-2007, 15.9% in 2008-2015 and 23.2% in 2016-2026. The Nasdaq version made **−16.1% a year in 1999-2007**, then 16.3% and 30.3% ([coordinator backtest](../research/classic.py)). A longer simulation back to 1928 confirms that the filter is not a safety net against everything. The 3x S&P with the filter still fell **93.5% in 1929-1935**, and the 3x Nasdaq with the filter fell 96% in 2000-2003, whipsawing 34 times as bear-market rallies pulled it back in ([notes backtest output](../research_notes/Small%20account%20trading%20strategy%20evidence/backtest_code/trend_leverage_backtest_output.txt)). The filter protects against slow bear markets. Fast crashes such as 1987 and March 2020 hit before it can react.

The approach also suits the bot's limitations. It checks prices once a day and switches about 5-6 times a year. Even at the bot's current 0.3% slippage per trade, that costs under 2% a year, against about 200% for daily round trips. Delayed data does not matter much for a daily rule, and GitHub Actions running a few minutes late does not matter either.

## Four strategies worth testing, ranked, with exact rules

The ranking weighs three things: how much independent evidence supports the rule, the chance of reaching $1,000 within a few years, and the chance of a crippling loss on the way. The first three use the same skeleton and differ only in what they hold.

**Common rules for strategies 1-3.**

| Step | Rule |
|---|---|
| Signal data | One daily closing price for the signal ETF (SPY or QQQ). Any source works, including Alpaca's free real-time IEX feed ([Alpaca data docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/about-market-data-api)). |
| Signal | After each close, compute the 200-day simple moving average: the average of the last 200 daily closes. **In** if today's close is above it, **out** if below. |
| When to trade | The next trading day, once, at about 3:45 PM New York time. Both backtests support a one-day lag: the coordinator's acted at the signal-day close, the notes' backtest at the next day's close, and the results were similar. |
| Order | Marketable limit order (current ask + 0.1% to buy, bid − 0.1% to sell), fractional shares, time-in-force Day. If unfilled after 5 minutes, re-price once. Never a plain market order. |
| Size | All of the account's *settled* cash when in (keep about $2 back so the order isn't rejected); 0% when out. No stop-loss, no profit target, no intraday checks. |
| Cash rule | Buy only with settled cash. In a cash account, sale proceeds settle the next business day, and buying with unsettled money can trigger good-faith violations. |
| Expected activity | About 5-6 switches a year (two or three round trips). Many of them will be small losing whipsaws, which is normal. |

**1. SPY 200-day trend into UPRO (3x S&P 500). Best overall.** The signal comes from SPY, and the bot holds UPRO when in and cash when out. This rule has the strongest evidence chain: trend following itself is independently replicated, Gayed and Bilello added leverage, and two separate backtests here agree. It also has the lowest chance of crippling losses among the options that can realistically reach 5x. Expect about **16% a year over the long run**, a worst drop of **−71%**, a 15% chance of $1,000 within five years, an 8% chance of being halved within five years, and a median wait of about 8 years. With real UPRO prices, 2010-2026, it made 21.0% a year with a −51% worst drop ([notes backtest output](../research_notes/Small%20account%20trading%20strategy%20evidence/backtest_code/trend_leverage_backtest_output.txt)).

**2. QQQ 200-day trend into TQQQ (3x Nasdaq-100). Higher reward, higher risk.** The signal comes from QQQ, and the bot holds TQQQ when in. Its chance of $1,000 within five years is about double strategy 1's (30%), and so is its chance of being halved (16%). Since 2010 it has looked spectacular: 28.7% a year with real TQQQ through November 2026 ([notes backtest output](../research_notes/Small%20account%20trading%20strategy%20evidence/backtest_code/trend_leverage_backtest_output.txt)). But that window is the best Nasdaq run on record, and the rule became popular because of it. The full history includes a nine-year stretch of losses (−16% a year) and a −95% drop. Calendar years since 2010 include −33.8% (2011), −42.2% (2022) and −17.4% (2026 so far). A 50/50 split between strategies 1 and 2 is a reasonable middle ground, but neither backtest tested it.

**3. SPY 200-day trend, 1x (plain SPY). The safe benchmark.** The signal comes from SPY, and the bot holds SPY when in. It made 8.1% a year with a −29% worst drop and a slightly better Sharpe than buy-and-hold, 0.71 against 0.63 ([coordinator backtest](../research/classic.py)). At this pace a 5x takes about 20 years. It belongs on the list because any bot that cannot beat it after costs is not worth its risk, and because it is the right choice if a −70% drop would be unbearable.

**4. Overnight QQQ. A test with paper money only, and probably dropped afterwards.** Buy QQQ with a marketable limit order at about 3:58 PM, sell at about 9:32 AM the next trading day, every day. With zero costs it had the best risk-adjusted record here (13.8% a year, Sharpe 0.97, worst drop −34%). It survives only if all-in costs stay under about **0.005% a side**, half a basis point. The bot's measured slippage is 50-100 times that, fractional orders can't use the auctions, and Alpaca's paper account doesn't simulate auction prices faithfully ([Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/frustration-with-the-execution-of-pre-market-orders/13787)). The only value of testing it is to measure real fill quality. If 60+ live round trips with a few dollars average more than 0.005% a side, it should be dropped.

The bot should stop live trading the opening-range breakout, the swing pullback rules, intraday momentum, reversal and earnings-drift strategies. None of them has a demonstrated edge larger than the bot's costs.

## The honest forecast: roughly one in seven within five years

Using strategy 1, the realistic outlook for $200→$1,000 without new deposits looks like this. Within one year the historical chance is **essentially zero** (0% of starting days for 3x S&P with the filter) ([notes backtest output](../research_notes/Small%20account%20trading%20strategy%20evidence/backtest_code/trend_leverage_backtest_output.txt)). Within three years it is about **1 in 17** (6%). Within five years it is about **1 in 7** (15%). It becomes more likely than not only around **8 to 10 years**. Along the way, the bot owner should expect at least one stretch where the account falls by half or more from its high, and about a 1 in 12 chance of the balance itself dropping to $100 within the first five years. Strategy 2 changes the odds to about **1 in 3 within five years**, with **1 in 6 of being halved** and a real possibility of a lost decade.

Treat these as upper estimates, for four reasons. First, the history is mostly the US stock market's very strong run, especially 2016-2026, and the Nasdaq is studied precisely because it was the index that won. Second, the backtests ignore taxes, and before 2009-2010 they rely on simulated funds. Third, a single day where the index falls a third would wipe out a 3x fund, and that has not happened in the sample. Fourth, several Alpaca details were not confirmed: whether UPRO and TQQQ can be bought in fractions, whether a leveraged-ETF acknowledgement is required, and how good-faith violations are handled. A test week in the paper account answers these. The pattern-day-trader rule is no longer the constraint it was: FINRA removed the $25,000 minimum from June 2026 ([FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-10](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/notices/26-10)). Margin still needs $2,000, so the account stays at 1x and leverage has to come from the fund itself.

Deposits change the picture completely. $200 a month reaches $1,000 in four months at any return. With a trend bot running underneath, the deposits themselves start to grow. Measured against that, the strategy's job is to grow savings steadily over years. It cannot turn a small stake into a big one in a hurry.

## Conclusion

The research points to a different target: a strategy that survives trading costs and slow automation and waits through long bear markets in cash. Fast breakouts fail on costs and timing, and calendar tricks are too small to matter. A daily trend signal on a 3x index fund trades about six times a year, so the bot's slow data and slippage stop mattering. It is the one approach where a small automated account has the same edge as a large one. The price is a −70% drawdown, which the owner has to be willing to sit through.

The next useful step is not another rule tweak. It is to run strategy 1 (and, if desired, 2) in paper trading alongside the 1x benchmark for a few months. That will confirm the Alpaca mechanics and the real fill costs, and the backtest numbers can then be checked against live behaviour before the real $200 goes in. Anyone promising $50 to $1,000 in weeks is showing the 1-in-20 winner of a coin-flip contest.
